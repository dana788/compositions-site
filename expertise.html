Expertise

Not every organizational problem belongs here. These five do.

Compositions works on commitments where communication and organizational reality have to hold together — and where the gap between what's promised and what's delivered carries real consequences. Below is what's typically at stake in each, and what tends to change once it's addressed.

What should guide the organization's decisions, conduct, and identity?

When values guide decisions in the moments that count — not just the moments people are watching — the organization becomes easier to trust, easier to follow, and easier to hold accountable.

Signals You May Recognize

  • "Our values feel generic."
  • "Leadership isn't aligned on what the purpose means."
  • "Employees can name the values, but they don't use them."

What Changes

  • Decisions leadership can defend, not just describe
  • A shared standard for judgment calls under pressure
  • Language people actually use, not language they recite
  • A values statement that holds up under a hard decision

Does the employee promise match the experience and capability the organization creates?

When what you promise employees matches what they actually experience, retention, referrals, and manager credibility stop being separate problems you're solving one at a time.

Signals You May Recognize

  • "The EVP doesn't sound like the actual experience."
  • "We're asking managers to carry too much."
  • "The same workforce concern keeps appearing in different forms."

What Changes

  • A workforce promise the organization can actually keep
  • Managers equipped with authority to match their responsibility
  • Fewer relaunches of the same culture initiative
  • A clearer read on what's actually driving retention and disengagement

Can the organization consistently sustain what customers have been promised?

A brand promise the organization can consistently deliver builds pricing power and referrals. One it can't becomes a liability every time a customer notices the gap.

Signals You May Recognize

  • "The campaign is stronger than the experience."
  • "Different parts of the organization deliver the brand differently."
  • "Our external narrative and internal reality are drifting apart."

What Changes

  • A promise every function is actually equipped to keep
  • Consistency across the touchpoints customers notice most
  • Marketing and operations working from the same definition of the brand
  • A narrative the organization can stand behind under scrutiny

Are people only informed about the change — or able and supported to enact it?

Adoption that holds — not just a well-attended launch — is what determines whether a transformation investment pays for itself.

Signals You May Recognize

  • "People understand the change, but they're not working differently."
  • "The launch went well, but adoption has stalled."
  • "We keep relaunching the same change."

What Changes

  • Adoption that holds after the launch excitement fades
  • Leaders equipped to model the change, not just announce it
  • One relaunch instead of three
  • A clear read on where the change is taking hold and where it isn't

What changed, for whom, and what can the organization responsibly claim?

A public commitment you can defend with evidence protects credibility. One you can't becomes the story that gets told about you instead of the one you intended to tell.

Signals You May Recognize

  • "We have a lot of activity but can't clearly explain the impact."
  • "Our public commitment is stronger than our evidence."
  • "The report describes what we did, not what changed."

What Changes

  • Claims the organization can defend under scrutiny
  • A clear account of what changed, for whom
  • Impact communication grounded in evidence, not activity counts
  • Stronger footing with funders, partners, and community stakeholders

Whatever the starting point, the response is shaped by what you're facing — not a fixed package.

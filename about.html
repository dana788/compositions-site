About Compositions

The commitment still isn't holding.

The Practice

Every commitment has conditions.

A commitment isn't only a statement of intent. It creates requirements — resources to allocate, authority to clarify, incentives to align, evidence to produce — and it depends on whether the organization surrounding it reinforces or quietly contradicts it.

Organizations invest heavily in culture, change, employer brand, leadership, and people commitments. The intentions are usually genuine. The initiatives are usually well designed. Most of that investment still doesn't produce the credibility, adoption, trust, or return leaders expected.

The instinct is to build another program, or to improve the message — better language, stronger storytelling, more leadership visibility. That instinct isn't wrong. It's usually incomplete.

A commitment can be sincere and still be structurally unsupported.

Every day, HR and people leaders invest in employer brand and culture commitments, communications leaders build change narratives, and executives make public claims — with real intent and real craft. Those commitments are structured as programs: well-designed, resourced, and launched with organizational support. But programs cannot substitute for the conditions required to sustain them.

Compositions examines the relationship between what an organization commits to and the structural conditions responsible for carrying it — governance, incentives, decision rights, leadership behavior, and the evidence available to confirm it — before the distance between what's said and what's true becomes damage.

The Work

Not a communications agency. Not a culture consultancy. Not a general management consultancy.

A structural advisory practice for organizations where investments in culture, change, brand, leadership, and people commitments are not producing the credibility, adoption, trust, or measurable return leaders expected.

The issue is rarely the design of the initiative or the clarity of the message. It's the gap between what the organization has committed to and what its structural conditions can actually sustain.

The visible concern is the entry point.
The work is structural.
The governing question is whether the organization is arranged to deliver what it says it will.

The deliverables are written structural findings, executive decision briefs, and — where scoped — governance design: built for the organization's own team to carry forward, not to create dependency on continued outside support.

Why Compositions

The name holds two meanings. Both describe the same problem.

In writing, music, and design, a composition isn't a collection of elements — it's the intentional arrangement of elements into a unified whole. The quality of the composition isn't only determined by the quality of the individual pieces. It's determined by how they work together.

Organizations are compositions. They're arrangements of commitments, governance, incentives, communications, and behavior. When those elements are arranged coherently — when the culture narrative aligns with what gets rewarded, when the employer brand describes an experience the operating conditions actually produce — the whole becomes credible.

When the elements contradict each other, the organization fragments. Each part may make sense on its own. The contradiction appears in composition.

The Second Meaning

A composition, in this context, is where structure meets commitment — the relationship between what the organization is actually arranged to deliver and what it says it will.

Reading that relationship, identifying where it has separated, and clarifying what leadership must govern for coherence to hold is the work.

The entry point is whatever's visible: the employer brand that isn't converting, the change that isn't holding, the message that keeps getting harder to write. The structural source sits beneath it. That's where the work goes.

The Foundation

Seven lenses. One integrated way of reading an organization.

The distinction isn't that any one of these disciplines is unusual on its own. It's that most practitioners hold one or two of them. Compositions is built from all seven at once — which is why the structural read it produces traces a full line from commitment to structure to experience to evidence to consequence, rather than stopping at any single function's view of the problem.

01

Strategic Communications at Enterprise Scale

Reads the gap between what an organization says, what its operating decisions confirm, and what people actually experience. Communications is treated as evidence — a message that keeps getting harder to write is often revealing a commitment the structure isn't yet built to prove.

02

Brand Strategy

Reads whether a brand promise — including the employer brand — is structurally real, or performing a reality the organization can't currently sustain. A brand refresh can sharpen the language. It can't make an unsupported promise credible.

03

Organizational Development at Enterprise Scale

Reads the conditions shaping behavior at scale — decision rights, accountability, capability, role clarity. Grounded in director-level work inside complex, multi-office organizations, not theory.

04

Culture Integration at Enterprise Scale

Reads what an organization's operating decisions, incentives, and leadership behavior actually reinforce — or quietly contradict. Culture as the cumulative result of what gets rewarded, funded, and tolerated, not a stated value.

05

Impact & Outcomes Measurement

Reads whether a commitment is producing evidence that can be responsibly defended — distinguishing activity from outcome, and directional exposure from confirmed loss. This is what makes a structural finding legible in the language of capital and governance.

06

Social Epidemiology & Structural-Determinant Thinking

Grounds the work in a simple premise: outcomes are shaped by the conditions surrounding people, not intention alone. The same reasoning that explains population-level health outcomes explains why a well-intentioned commitment can still fail to hold.

07

Instructional Design & Permanent Capability Transfer

Ensures a finding becomes usable, governable capability — not a dependency on continued outside interpretation. The engagement ends. The organization owns what it learned.

Dana Middleton | Structural Advisor, Compositions

Across more than two decades of watching well-designed programs and well-crafted communications repeatedly fail to hold, one contrast kept recurring.

Not:

"How do we communicate this commitment better?"

But: "What conditions would need to be true for this commitment to be credible?"

Not because anyone made a bad decision, and not because one function was out of step with another. The structural conditions beneath a funded commitment are rarely examined before the commitment is made public — which is exactly why the same pattern shows up decades and industries apart: the initiative is sound, the message lands, and the commitment still doesn't hold.

That question sharpened building the infrastructure required to make culture, change, and people commitments structurally real, across complex, multi-office environments — and navigating the specific gap between what leadership intends to commit to and what the organization's operating conditions can actually sustain.

The answer kept pointing to the same place: governance, incentives, decision rights, and operating behavior — the conditions beneath the communications layer, not the communications themselves. That's where Compositions operates.

Dana Middleton is a Structural Advisor and the principal of Compositions, a Kansas City-based advisory practice. Her work sits at the intersection of strategic communications, brand architecture, organizational development, culture integration, impact measurement, and social epidemiology — not as separate specialties, but as one integrated way of reading an organization.

With more than two decades of experience across strategic communications, brand strategy, and enterprise-scale culture integration — including work inside a 550-plus employee global marketing organization and an approximately 1,800-employee health insurance organization — and doctoral research examining coherence as a structural determinant of stakeholder wellbeing and enterprise health, she brings the pattern recognition of a practitioner who has been inside the gap and built the infrastructure to close it.

The people who can most precisely name the structural source of a credibility gap are rarely the ones with the authority to close it alone. Dana has spent her career in that position — and now brings the structural lens that changes what's possible from it.

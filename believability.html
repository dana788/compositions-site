Believability

Believability isn't about what you said. It's about what you've shown people when it actually cost you something.

You made a commitment — to your culture, your customers, your community, your team. You built the program, sent the communications, ran the launch. Believability is the question underneath all of it: have you given people real reason to believe it's true, or does it just look like you have?

See sessions & formats Tell us about your room

Most leadership training on trust starts and ends with communication — say it better, say it more often. Believability starts one layer deeper.

The Composition of Believability

Four questions. Whichever one goes unanswered is usually where the commitment breaks.

Sometimes the fix really is the message — sharper, clearer, better timed. Just as often, it's something underneath it: who has the authority to act, what's actually being rewarded, or whether the experience holds up the same way for everyone the commitment was made to, not just the people closest to power.

Commitment

Was this ever defined clearly enough to check?

A commitment that sounds resonant can still be too vague to test against evidence later. Precision here is what makes everything downstream possible to diagnose at all.

Conditions

Did we build the budget, authority, and systems to deliver it?

Intention doesn't deliver a commitment. Resourcing, authority, and incentives do — independent of how sincerely it was meant.

Proof

Did people see real evidence they could verify for themselves?

Good work that never crosses the boundary into someone's actual experience isn't proof yet — it's still just activity.

Trust

Does all of that add up to something people actually believe?

Trust is the readout, not the lever. When it's missing, the fix is almost never more communication — it's tracing back to whichever pillar broke.

When one of these is underdeveloped, a gap appears — the specific, namable distance between what people are asked to believe and what they've actually been given reason to believe. This isn't built on the assumption that something failed. It starts from a narrower, more useful question: which of the four hasn't been built yet, and what would it take to build it.

An engagement score that looks fine on average can still mean a commitment is working well for some groups and quietly failing others — and that distinction is often exactly what separates an initiative that keeps its funding from one that quietly loses support next cycle.

Dana Middleton, Structural Communications Advisor & Principal, Compositions
